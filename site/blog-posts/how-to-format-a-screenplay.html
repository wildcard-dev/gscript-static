How to Format a Screenplay: The Complete Guide

G-Script Team
August 11, 2026
GUIDE

If you've ever opened a blank page and wondered whether your scene heading should be capitalized, whether "INT." needs a period, or how far to indent dialogue — you're not alone, and you're not overthinking it. Screenplay format isn't a style preference. It's a shared visual language that tells a reader (or a director, or a script supervisor) exactly what they're looking at within about two seconds of glancing at the page.

Get it wrong and the read gets harder — sometimes hard enough that a script gets passed over before anyone's judged a single line of your story. Get it right and the format disappears, which is the whole point: it should never be the thing a reader notices.

This guide covers everything you need to format a screenplay correctly, from scene headings to title pages, plus the page-count rules of thumb that keep your script paced like an industry-standard one. At the end, you can grab a free one-page cheat sheet and a ready-to-use Google Docs template so you don't have to hold all of this in your head while you write.

What "industry-standard format" actually means (and why it matters)

Screenplay format exists because a script isn't just a story — it's a production document. One formatted page roughly equals one minute of screen time, which is how directors, producers, and financiers estimate runtime and budget before a single frame is shot. Courier (or a Courier-style monospaced font) at 12pt isn't an aesthetic throwback; it's what makes that one-page-per-minute math hold up.

The practical upshot for you: a reader who's seen thousands of scripts can tell within a page or two whether you know the format. If you don't, it reads as a signal that you're new — which might cost you the benefit of the doubt on everything else, including a story that's actually good. Formatting correctly doesn't get your script bought. But formatting incorrectly can get it set aside before that's even a question.

The core elements of screenplay format

Every screenplay is built from a small, consistent set of elements. Once you know them, you can format anything.

ElementWhat it doesFormatting rule
Scene heading (slugline)Establishes location and timeALL CAPS: INT. or EXT. + location + — DAY / — NIGHT
Action lineDescribes what's visible on screenPresent tense, single-spaced, full width of the margin
Character cueAnnounces who's speakingALL CAPS, centered roughly 3.7" from the left margin
DialogueWhat the character saysCentered under the cue, narrower margins (~2.5" from each side)
ParentheticalA brief acting/tone directionIn parentheses, directly under the character cue, used sparingly
TransitionSignals a cut or editALL CAPS, right-aligned (e.g., CUT TO:) — used sparingly in modern scripts

A quick example, formatted the way it would appear on the page:

INT. COFFEE SHOP - DAY

MAYA sits alone at a corner table, laptop open, coffee
untouched. She checks her phone. Nothing.

The door chimes. She doesn't look up.

                    MAYA
          (not looking up)
          You're late.

                    DEV
          Traffic. You look terrible.

                    MAYA
          Thank you.

A few rules worth internalizing early:

  • Scene headings always start with INT. (interior) or EXT. (exterior), followed by the specific location, followed by the time of day — most commonly DAY or NIGHT. Keep it as short as it can be while staying specific: INT. MAYA'S APARTMENT - KITCHEN - NIGHT beats a full sentence.
  • Action lines are written in present tense, because the reader is watching the story happen now, not remembering it. Keep them lean — dense paragraphs of description are one of the fastest ways to lose a reader's pace.
  • Character cues are capitalized every time a character speaks, even if they were just mentioned in the action line above.
  • Parentheticals are for brief tone or delivery notes ((sarcastic), (to Dev)) — not for stage direction. If it takes more than 2-3 words, it usually belongs in an action line instead.

Title page format (with example)

Every script opens with a clean, simple title page — no artwork, no fancy fonts, no synopsis. Just:




                         THE LONG WAY HOME

                              Written by

                            Maya Chen




                                                    Maya Chen
                                                    maya.chen@email.com
                                                    (555) 012-3456

The title is centered, roughly a third of the way down the page, often in all caps or title case (either is accepted). "Written by" sits just beneath it, followed by the writer's name. Contact information — email and phone, sometimes a WGA registration or copyright number — goes in the bottom-right corner. That's it. A title page that tries to do more than this reads as a red flag, not a highlight.

Page count and pacing (the 1-page-≈-1-minute rule)

Because one page of correctly formatted screenplay equals roughly one minute of screen time, page count is a proxy for runtime:

  • Feature film: typically 90–120 pages (a 100–110 page script is the safest range for a first feature)
  • TV drama (hour-long): roughly 55–65 pages
  • TV comedy (half-hour): roughly 25–35 pages
  • Short film: as short as the story needs — usually under 15–20 pages

This is exactly why format matters beyond aesthetics: if your margins, font, or spacing drift from standard, your page count stops meaning anything. A script that's "wrong-formatted" at 100 pages might actually run 140 minutes on screen — and an experienced reader will clock that within the first few pages, before they've read a word of your story.

Common formatting mistakes that get scripts passed over

  • Wrong font or size. Anything other than 12pt Courier (or Courier Prime, its more legible modern cousin) throws off the page-to-minute math and looks amateur at a glance.
  • Camera directions everywhere. Modern spec scripts avoid PAN TO:, CLOSE ON:, and similar directing notes — that's the director's job. Let the action line imply it.
  • Overwritten action lines. Dense blocks of description slow the read and usually signal a writer who hasn't yet learned to trust the visual medium.
  • Inconsistent scene headings. If a location is INT. KITCHEN - DAY in scene 3, it shouldn't become INT. THE KITCHEN - DAYTIME in scene 12. Readers notice the drift even if they can't name why something feels off.
  • Skipping the title page, or overdesigning it. Both extremes read the same way to an experienced reader: unfamiliarity with the format.

Do it by hand vs. use a tool

You can format a screenplay by hand — setting margins, tabs, and paragraph styles manually in whatever word processor you already use. Plenty of working writers did exactly that for decades. The tradeoff is that it's easy to drift out of format as you write and edit, especially across a long revision process, and re-checking every scene heading and character cue by eye gets old fast.

The other end of the spectrum — dedicated screenwriting software like Final Draft — solves the drift problem, but it also means leaving the place you're probably already writing everything else: Google Docs. That's a real cost if you're collaborating with a co-writer, a teacher, or a friend giving notes, since it means abandoning the commenting, version history, and real-time editing you already rely on.
