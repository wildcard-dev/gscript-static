How Many Pages Is a Screenplay?

G-Script Team
August 26, 2026
GUIDE

"How long should my script be?" is one of the first questions almost every writer asks, and the honest answer is: it depends what you're writing, but there's a rule that makes it easy to check yourself against. Screenplay page count isn't arbitrary: it's a direct stand-in for runtime, which means getting it right (or wrong) says something concrete about your script before anyone's read a word of the story.

Here's the rule, what "normal" looks like by format, and what it means if your draft is running long or short.

The rule: 1 page ≈ 1 minute of screen time

Properly formatted screenplay pages (12pt Courier, standard margins, standard spacing) run at roughly one page per minute of screen time. This isn't a style convention; it's the reason the format's font, margins, and spacing are standardized at all. A director scheduling a shoot, a producer estimating a budget, or a reader skimming a query all use page count as a fast proxy for runtime, and that only works if the formatting is consistent.

This is also why format matters beyond looking "correct": a script in the wrong font or with narrower margins can run twenty or thirty minutes longer on screen than its page count suggests. An experienced reader can often tell within the first few pages that the math doesn't add up.

Page count by format

FormatTypical page countRuntime
Feature film90–120 pages90–120 minutes
Feature film (first script, safest range)100–110 pages~100–110 minutes
TV drama (hour-long)55–65 pages~45–60 minutes (with ad breaks)
TV comedy (half-hour)25–35 pages~22–30 minutes (with ad breaks)
Short filmUnder 15–20 pagesUnder 15–20 minutes
Streaming drama (no ad breaks)45–65 pagesVaries more than broadcast TV: check the specific network/platform's norms

A few nuances worth knowing:

  • Comedies tend to run shorter than dramas at every length: a feature comedy sitting at 95 pages is unremarkable; a drama at 95 pages is on the lean side.
  • First-time feature writers should aim for the 100–110 page range specifically, not just "under 120." Scripts that open at 140+ pages read as a pacing red flag before the story is even considered.
  • TV page counts vary more by network/platform than features do: if you're writing a spec for a specific show, check recent produced scripts in that format rather than relying on the general range alone.

Why page count depends on getting the format right

The 1-page-≈-1-minute rule only holds if the underlying formatting (font, margins, line spacing) is standard. This is the part that trips people up: two scripts can have the exact same word count and land at wildly different page counts if one is formatted correctly and the other isn't.

The formatting choices that most affect page count:

  • Font and size: anything other than 12pt Courier (or Courier Prime) throws off the page-to-minute math immediately.
  • Margins: narrower margins fit more text per page, silently deflating your page count relative to actual runtime.
  • Action line density: long, unbroken paragraphs of description compress more story into fewer pages than the format intends, which also tends to slow the read.

What to do if your script is running long (or short)

If you're over the typical range for your format:

  • Check formatting first: wrong font or margins can add 10-20+ "phantom" pages before you've cut a single scene.
  • Look for overwritten action lines. Dense description is one of the most common places to trim without losing story.
  • Ask whether every scene is earning its place, or whether two scenes are doing the job of one.

If you're under the typical range:

  • Make sure you're not under-describing key moments: sparse action lines can be under-formatted just as easily as they can be efficient.
  • A short first draft isn't a problem on its own; it's common to add scenes and beats in revision. It only becomes a concern if the story feels rushed or incomplete as written.
