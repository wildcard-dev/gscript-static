How to Format Dialogue in a Script

G-Script Team
August 22, 2026
GUIDE

Dialogue is where screenplay format gets the most exceptions: extensions like (V.O.) and (CONT'D), when to use a parenthetical versus just trusting the actor, how to handle two people talking over each other. Get the broad strokes of screenplay format right and dialogue is usually where a script still looks a little off.

This guide covers dialogue formatting specifically: the anatomy of a dialogue block, the extensions you'll actually need, and the mistakes that make dialogue-heavy pages harder to read than they should be. For the full picture of screenplay format (scene headings, action lines, title pages), see our complete guide, How to Format a Screenplay: The Complete Guide.

The anatomy of a dialogue block

Every line of spoken dialogue is built from up to four parts, always in this order:

                    CHARACTER
          (parenthetical)
          Dialogue line goes here, centered
          under the character cue.
  1. Character cue: ALL CAPS, centered roughly 3.7" from the left margin, naming who's speaking.
  2. Parenthetical (optional): a brief tone or delivery note in parentheses, directly under the cue.
  3. Dialogue: the actual line, in sentence case, centered in a narrower column (~2.5" margins on each side) than action lines.
  4. Extension (optional): a tag like (V.O.) appended to the character cue itself, not a separate line.

Character cue extensions you'll actually use

A handful of standard tags cover almost every situation:

ExtensionMeansExample
(V.O.)Voice-over: the character isn't physically present (narration, phone call heard but not seen)MAYA (V.O.)
(O.S.)Off-screen: the character is in the scene but not in frameDEV (O.S.)
(CONT'D)The same character is continuing to speak after an action line interrupted themMAYA (CONT'D)
(O.C.)Off-camera: used interchangeably with O.S. in some house styles; pick one and stay consistentDEV (O.C.)

A common beginner mistake is reaching for (V.O.) when (O.S.) is correct, or vice versa: if the character is in the room but just not currently on screen, that's off-screen, not voice-over. Voice-over is for narration, phone calls, letters being read aloud, or a character speaking from somewhere entirely outside the scene.

Parentheticals: what they're for, and when to skip them

A parenthetical is a short acting note (tone, target, or a physical beat that affects how the line is delivered):

                    MAYA
          (not looking up)
          You're late.

The rule that keeps parentheticals useful: 2-3 words, used sparingly. They're for information the actor genuinely needs and that isn't obvious from context, not a running director's commentary on every line. If you find yourself writing (sarcastic), (angrily), and (with a sigh) on three consecutive lines, that's usually a sign the dialogue itself isn't doing enough work, or that you don't trust the actor to find the tone. Well-written dialogue rarely needs more than one parenthetical every few pages.

Formatting a back-and-forth exchange

Here's a full exchange, formatted the way it would appear on the page:

INT. COFFEE SHOP - DAY

MAYA sits alone at a corner table, laptop open, coffee
untouched.

                    MAYA
          (not looking up)
          You're late.

                    DEV (O.S.)
          Traffic. You look terrible.

MAYA finally looks up as DEV drops into the chair across
from her.

                    MAYA
          Thank you.

Notice that DEV (O.S.) is used because Dev is speaking before he's fully in frame; the moment he sits down and is visibly in the scene, later cues would just read DEV, no extension needed.

Dual dialogue (two characters talking at once)

For simultaneous dialogue (rare, but occasionally necessary), most screenwriting software and formatting tools support a side-by-side dual-dialogue block, with both character cues at the same vertical position and each column running independently underneath. If you're formatting by hand, this is one of the few elements genuinely difficult to get right without a tool built for the format: it's a good sign you've reached for a feature worth letting software handle.

Common dialogue formatting mistakes

  • Parenthetical overload. A tone note on every single line reads as a lack of trust in the actor and slows the read.
  • Wrong extension. Mixing up (V.O.) and (O.S.), or skipping (CONT'D) when an action line splits a character's speech.
  • Dialogue that isn't centered/narrowed. Full-width dialogue (the same margins as an action line) is one of the fastest visual tells of an off-spec script.
  • Inconsistent character cue capitalization. MAYA in one scene, Maya in another; pick one and never deviate.
  • Interrupting a character's own line with a new cue instead of (CONT'D). If Maya's line is broken by an action beat but she's still the one talking, the second half needs MAYA (CONT'D), not a fresh, unlabeled cue.
